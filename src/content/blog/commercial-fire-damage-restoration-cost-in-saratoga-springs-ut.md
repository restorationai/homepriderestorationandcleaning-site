## What drives the cost up or down for a business?

Square footage and construction type set the floor, but five other factors move a commercial fire estimate more than anything else. Knowing them helps a business owner sanity-check a scope before signing off.

- **HVAC contamination.** Smoke travels through return air ducts far beyond the room where the fire started. A single-zone system in a small suite might need duct cleaning for $3,000-$5,000; a rooftop unit serving a strip mall or warehouse can push that past $10,000.
- **Inventory and equipment.** Retail stock, restaurant equipment, and office electronics are either cleaned and restored or written off as a total loss. A specialty cleaning pass on restorable inventory usually costs a fraction of replacement value, but soot-contaminated electronics and perishable stock typically can't be saved.
- **Structural vs. smoke-only damage.** Char damage to framing, roof decking, or load-bearing walls means demolition and reconstruction, which is the single biggest cost driver on any commercial loss.
- **After-hours or expedited work.** Running crews nights and weekends to reopen faster typically adds a premium to labor, but it can be cheaper than the revenue lost from staying closed longer.
- **Code-required upgrades.** If the building department requires updated fire-rated assemblies, sprinkler modifications, or electrical upgrades during rebuild, that adds cost beyond simple restoration.

The restoration crew follows the [ANSI/IICRC S700 standard for fire and smoke restoration](https://iicrc.org/s700/) regardless of building size, which is why a proper scope documents every affected system, not just the room with visible char.

## Does commercial property insurance cover fire damage?

Most commercial property policies cover fire damage to the structure and the contents listed in the policy, but business interruption coverage, the part that replaces lost income while the doors are closed, is often a separate rider or endorsement, not an automatic feature. Check the declarations page for a "business income" or "extra expense" section before assuming it's included.

Documentation protects the claim either way. Photograph everything before anything moves, including inventory, equipment serial numbers, and structural damage. Keep a log of lost revenue days and any extra expenses, like renting temporary space or expediting materials, since many business income policies reimburse those too. The [Insurance Information Institute's guide to settling claims after a disaster](https://www.iii.org/article/settling-insurance-claims-after-a-disaster) walks through what adjusters typically expect from a documented loss, and it applies to commercial claims as much as residential ones.

## What does the restoration process look like for a business?

A commercial fire restoration follows the same core sequence as a residential one, scaled up, with more emphasis on getting the business back open.

1. **Emergency board-up and stabilization.** Secure openings, tarp roof damage, and stop additional loss from weather or security exposure.
2. **Assessment and documentation.** Walk the space, photograph damage room by room, and build the written scope the insurer will reference.
3. **Water extraction and content pack-out.** If sprinklers or hose lines ran, standing water comes out first. Salvageable inventory and equipment are packed out for off-site cleaning.
4. **Soot and smoke removal, HVAC decontamination.** Structural surfaces are cleaned, ductwork is serviced, and air scrubbers run to clear residual odor and particulate.
5. **Structural drying and reconstruction.** Framing, drywall, flooring, and any code-required upgrades get rebuilt to current standards.
6. **Final walkthrough and reoccupancy.** This step is often scheduled after hours or on a weekend specifically to shorten the gap before the business reopens.
