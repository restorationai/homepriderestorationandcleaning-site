## What drives the price up or down?

The price moves most on how long the odor has had to settle and how much of the home it's reached, not just the square footage.

Factors that raise the cost:

- **Source and duration.** A one-time grease fire smells very different from 15 years of indoor cigarette smoke. Longer exposure means odor has worked into porous materials (carpet pad, insulation, subfloor) that may need replacing rather than treating.
- **HVAC involvement.** If the furnace ran during or after the smoke event, odor compounds are now coating every duct run in the house. Skipping duct cleaning is the most common reason a treated home smells like smoke again within weeks.
- **Occupied vs. vacant.** Ozone generators produce a gas that's unsafe to breathe, so the property must sit empty for the treatment window. Hydroxyl generators are occupied-safe but work more slowly, which usually means more billable hours.

- **Porous contents.** Upholstered furniture, drapes, and clothing often need separate deodorization or disposal, which adds to the total beyond the structural treatment.

## Ozone vs. hydroxyl vs. thermal fogging: which method costs more, and why?

Ozone treatment is usually the cheapest per session because it works fast, but it only works on a vacant property and can't be run while people, pets, or plants are inside. Hydroxyl treatment costs more because it runs safely around occupants but needs more hours of generator time to reach the same result. Thermal fogging sits in between: it's a targeted, same-day treatment best suited to a single room or a specific odor pocket rather than a whole house.

Many restoration companies, including our team, combine methods rather than picking just one. A typical whole-house job might start with thermal fogging to neutralize the heaviest pockets, add a hydroxyl generator run for occupied rooms, and finish with an HVAC pass so the duct system isn't just recirculating the smell. That combination approach is part of why the IICRC's [S700 Standard for Professional Fire and Smoke Damage Restoration](https://iicrc.org/s700/) treats odor control as a distinct phase of the restoration process, not an afterthought tacked onto cleaning.

For wildfire smoke that worked its way into a Saratoga Springs home through open windows or a running HVAC system during a smoky summer, the [EPA's guidance on wildfires and indoor air quality](https://www.epa.gov/emergencies-iaq/wildfires-and-indoor-air-quality-iaq) notes that fine smoke particles can linger in HVAC filters, upholstery, and ductwork well after the outdoor air clears. That's a case where duct cleaning plus a hydroxyl or ozone pass, rather than a single thermal fog treatment, usually gets the smell out.

## Will homeowners insurance cover smoke smell removal?

Most homeowners policies cover smoke odor removal when it results from a covered fire loss, since odor remediation is treated as part of the fire damage claim rather than a separate expense. If a kitchen fire, electrical fire, or similar sudden event triggered the smell, document it as part of that claim before any cleanup starts.

Cigarette smoke from a previous owner or tenant is a different situation. That odor didn't come from a covered peril, so it's not something a homeowner's insurance policy pays to remove. It's typically a negotiating point in a real estate transaction or a security-deposit dispute for a landlord, not an insurance claim. ## What happens during a professional smoke odor removal job?

A professional odor removal job follows a set sequence so the smell doesn't just get masked and come back in a few weeks.

1. **Inspection and odor source mapping.** A technician walks the property to identify where odor has concentrated: HVAC returns, soft materials, wall cavities near the original source.
2. **Removal of unsalvageable porous materials.** Carpet pad, damaged insulation, and heavily saturated drywall are removed rather than treated, since odor compounds are often too deep in these materials to neutralize.
3. **Cleaning hard surfaces.** Walls, trim, cabinetry, and hard flooring get wiped down with odor-neutralizing cleaners before any gas or vapor treatment starts.
4. **Odor treatment (ozone, hydroxyl, or thermal fogging).** The method is chosen based on whether the home is occupied and how deep the odor has settled, as described above.
5. **HVAC and duct treatment.** Filters are replaced, and ducts are cleaned or fogged so the system isn't recirculating smoke residue.
6. **Sealing and odor-blocking primer.** For stubborn cases, an odor-sealing primer goes on affected surfaces before repainting, locking in any residual odor molecules.
7. **Final odor verification.** A walkthrough (and sometimes an air-quality check) confirms the treatment held before the job is closed out.
