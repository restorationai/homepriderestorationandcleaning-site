## What certifications should a biohazard cleanup company have?

A legitimate biohazard cleanup company trains its technicians to the [IICRC S540 standard](https://iicrc.org/s540/) for trauma and crime scene cleanup, and complies with [OSHA's bloodborne pathogen rule](https://www.osha.gov/bloodborne-pathogens), which governs how workers handle, transport, and dispose of materials contaminated with blood or other potentially infectious material.

Ask directly: "Is your crew trained to the IICRC S540 standard?" and "How do you dispose of biohazard waste, and are you using a medical waste hauler?" A company that can't answer both clearly, or gets vague about disposal, is not equipped for this work. Biohazard waste can't go in a regular dumpster; it has to be manifested and transported by a medical waste service, the same chain of custody a hospital uses.

General IICRC certification (the umbrella credential covering water, fire, mold, and trauma disciplines) is a baseline worth asking about too. It signals the company has invested in formal training rather than learning procedures on the job.

## Why does discreet, unmarked response matter?

Discretion matters because the people hiring a biohazard cleanup company are almost always grieving, embarrassed, or dealing with neighbors and landlords who don't need to know the details. A company that rolls up with a giant branded wrap advertising "crime scene cleanup" on the side of a van in a quiet Saratoga Springs cul-de-sac is thinking about marketing, not about you.

The better pattern: plain or lightly marked vehicles, technicians in clean PPE rather than tactical-looking gear, and a crew that works the job without drawing a crowd. If you're interviewing companies, ask what their vehicles and crew look like on arrival. A good answer is short and boring, which is exactly what you want.

## What questions should you ask before hiring a biohazard cleanup company?

Five questions filter out most of the field fast:

1. **Are you available right now, or only during business hours?** Biohazard situations don't wait for Monday.
2. **What's your IICRC S540 and OSHA bloodborne pathogen training, specifically?** Vague answers are a red flag.
3. **Who hauls the waste, and is it a medical waste disposal service?** This is the step most unqualified companies skip or outsource poorly.
4. **Do you bill insurance or the state crime victim compensation program directly?** Many states, including Utah, offer compensation for trauma scene cleanup tied to a qualifying crime; a company that knows how to file that paperwork saves you money and stress.

5. **What does your crew wear on arrival, and how many vehicles show up?** You want a low-key answer, not a show of force.

If a company hesitates on the disposal question or can't name the standard they train to, keep calling.

## What does the biohazard cleanup process actually involve?

The work generally runs in four phases: scene assessment and containment, removal of contaminated and non-salvageable materials, cleaning and disinfection of everything that can be saved, and air quality verification before the space is handed back. A trauma scene involving flooring, drywall, or subfloor contamination usually means those materials come out entirely; porous surfaces that have absorbed blood or fluid can't be fully disinfected and are not treated as salvageable under the S540 standard.

After removal, every remaining surface gets cleaned with hospital-grade disinfectants and the space is deodorized, since biological odor tends to linger in HVAC systems and wall cavities long after visible contamination is gone.
