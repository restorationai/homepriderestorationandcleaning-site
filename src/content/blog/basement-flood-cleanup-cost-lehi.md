**TL;DR:** Basement flood cleanup in Lehi, UT typically costs $1,500 to $10,000 for a clean-water loss and $6,000 to $25,000 or more for a sewage backup, mainly because contaminated water forces removal of drywall, carpet, and padding that clean water does not. Extraction and drying alone usually run $3 to $8 per square foot; rebuild work (drywall, flooring, baseboard) is priced separately once the space is fully dry.

If your basement took on water overnight, either from a sump pump that couldn't keep up, a washer supply line, or a sewer line backing up through the floor drain, the first question is usually "what is this going to cost." The honest answer depends almost entirely on one thing: what kind of water it was, not how much square footage got wet. A finished Lehi basement near Traverse Mountain with carpet and drywall floods very differently than an unfinished daylight basement with a concrete floor and exposed framing.

## How Much Does Basement Flood Cleanup Cost in Lehi, UT?

Most Lehi homeowners pay between $1,500 and $10,000 for a clean-water basement flood and $6,000 to $25,000 or more when sewage is involved, with the final number driven by water category and finished square footage, not by the brand name on the invoice.

| Scenario | Typical Cost Range |
|---|---|
| Clean water (Category 1), partial basement, extraction + drying only | $1,500 - $3,500 |

| Gray water (Category 2) from a washer, dishwasher, or groundwater seepage | $4,000 - $9,000 |

These are typical industry ranges for the Lehi and broader Utah Valley area, not a quote. Every loss is different, and a written scope after an on-site inspection is the only way to know the real number for your basement.

## What Drives the Price Up or Down?

The single biggest cost driver is water category, under the [ANSI/IICRC S500 standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/): Category 1 (clean, from a supply line or water heater), Category 2 (gray water, like washing machine discharge or groundwater seeping through a foundation under hydrostatic pressure), or Category 3 (black water, which includes any sewer or floor-drain backup). Category 2 and 3 losses almost always require removing porous materials like carpet, pad, and the bottom few feet of drywall rather than drying them in place, which adds demolition and disposal cost that a Category 1 loss skips entirely.

Square footage and finish level matter next. An unfinished basement with a concrete slab and exposed studs is cheaper to dry and faster to clear, often needing only extraction and air movement. A finished basement with carpet, drywall, baseboard, and a bathroom adds both drying complexity and rebuild cost. Basements also fall under IICRC Class 4 drying in many cases, since concrete slabs and masonry foundation walls hold moisture deep in the material and take longer to dry than wood framing.

Response time is the factor homeowners control. Standing water that sits for more than 24 to 48 hours increases the odds of microbial growth, per the [EPA's guidance on mold and moisture](https://www.epa.gov/mold/brief-guide-mold-moisture-and-your-home), which can turn a straightforward drying job into a remediation job. Code-required upgrades (GFCI outlets, updated insulation) during rebuild can also add cost but aren't usually optional once walls are open.

## Will Insurance Cover Basement Flood Cleanup in Lehi?

Most standard homeowners policies cover sudden, accidental water damage, like a burst supply line or a water heater that fails without warning, but they typically exclude gradual seepage, flood water, and sump pump failure or sewer backup unless you've added a water backup endorsement. That endorsement is a separate line item on the policy, not something most homeowners have unless they specifically asked for it.

This is the detail that catches people off guard: a sewer backup through the floor drain is usually NOT covered by a basic homeowners policy. It's only covered when a water backup (sewer/sump) endorsement has been added. Surface flooding from outside the home, meanwhile, is a flood loss and needs a separate flood policy through the [National Flood Insurance Program](https://www.floodsmart.gov/) or a private flood carrier, not your homeowners policy at all. Our [full breakdown of what homeowners insurance covers for water damage](/blog/does-homeowners-insurance-cover-water-damage/) goes into more detail on the distinction.

Documentation protects whatever claim you do have. Photograph the standing water before anything is moved, keep every receipt, and get a written scope of work. ## What Happens During Basement Flood Cleanup?

The process runs in a predictable order: assess the water category, extract standing water, remove any materials that can't be saved, then dry and monitor the structure until moisture readings confirm it's done.

1. **Inspection and category identification.** A technician identifies whether the water is Category 1, 2, or 3, since that determines whether materials are dried in place or removed.
2. **Extraction.** Standing water is pumped and vacuumed out with truck-mounted or portable extraction equipment, usually within the first few hours.
3. **Demolition (Category 2/3 only).** Wet carpet, pad, and the lower section of drywall are cut out and disposed of when the water was contaminated.
4. **Drying.** Air movers and LGR dehumidifiers are set to dry framing, subfloor, and concrete, with daily moisture meter readings tracking progress toward dry standard.
5. **Antimicrobial treatment.** Any surfaces exposed to Category 2 or 3 water get treated to address bacteria before rebuild starts.
6. **Rebuild.** Once moisture readings confirm the structure is dry, drywall, flooring, and trim are reinstalled.

This is also where the line between cleanup and repair gets drawn for pricing purposes, since steps 1 through 5 are mitigation and step 6 is a separate rebuild scope.
