## What should you look for in a burst pipe cleanup company?

Look for four things: 24/7 availability, IICRC water damage certification, moisture-mapping equipment, and a process for documenting the loss for your insurer. A company missing any of these is a risk when you're trying to limit damage and get reimbursed.

24/7 matters because water doesn't wait for business hours. IICRC certification (the Institute of Inspection, Cleaning and Restoration Certification) means the technicians were trained to the [ANSI/IICRC S500 water damage restoration standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/), the industry benchmark for how structural drying is actually supposed to work. Moisture mapping, using penetrating and non-penetrating meters to find water that's migrated into walls or under flooring, is what separates a thorough dry-out from one that leaves hidden moisture behind to grow mold later.

## Should you call a plumber or a restoration company first?

You generally need both, but they do different jobs: a plumber stops the leak and repairs or replaces the broken pipe, while a water damage restoration company extracts the standing water, dries the structure, and documents the loss for insurance.

This is the part that trips people up. Home Pride Restoration, based in Saratoga Springs, is not a plumber and does not repair or replace pipes. What a crew like that handles is everything that happens after the water stops: pulling standing water, setting moisture-mapping readings, placing drying equipment, and building the documentation an adjuster will want to see. Our [burst pipe who-to-call guide](/blog/burst-pipe-in-saratoga-springs-who-to-call-first/) walks through the order of those two calls in more detail, and our [cost breakdown](/blog/burst-pipe-repair-cost-in-saratoga-springs-ut/) covers what each side of the job typically runs.

Timing matters because of how water is classified. A burst supply line usually starts as Category 1 (clean) water under the [ANSI/IICRC S500 standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/), but it doesn't stay clean once it sits on flooring, soaks into drywall, or mixes with dust and debris. The longer the delay between the burst and the first extraction call, the more likely that clean water becomes a bigger cleanup problem.

## What does a good cleanup crew actually do once the water is off?

A good crew extracts the standing water first, maps moisture behind walls and under flooring with meters, then places air movers and dehumidifiers sized to the affected area and tracks drying progress daily until readings hit dry standard.

Extraction comes first because wet building materials only start drying once the bulk water is gone. From there, moisture mapping tells the crew which drywall, baseboards, or subfloor are actually wet versus just damp on the surface, which matters because [mold can begin growing within 24 to 48 hours](https://www.epa.gov/mold/brief-guide-mold-moisture-and-your-home) on wet materials left untreated. Drying equipment then gets placed based on those readings, not guesswork, and a legitimate company checks and logs moisture levels daily rather than dropping off fans and disappearing until the invoice is due.

This is also where insurance documentation happens. Photos of the source, moisture readings over time, and a written scope of work are what support a claim later. Most homeowners policies cover sudden, accidental water damage from a burst pipe, though coverage for gradual leaks or pipes that failed from neglect is usually excluded, so [check what your policy actually covers](https://www.iii.org/article/what-is-covered-by-standard-homeowners-insurance) before assuming everything is paid.

## How do you vet credentials before letting a crew into your home?

Ask for IICRC certification numbers and how they handle insurance documentation before any equipment gets unloaded. A company with nothing to hide will answer both questions in under a minute.

Specifically, ask which IICRC category the technicians are certified in (water damage restoration, not just a general cleaning certification), whether they'll provide a written scope of work before starting, and whether they document moisture readings with photos and logs you can request a copy of. If a company hedges on any of those, or can't name a certification standard at all, keep looking. If they can't tell you who the plumber is that they're coordinating with, that's worth asking about too, since restoration and pipe repair should never be the same invoice line.
