## What actually drives the price?

The price moves on six things: square footage, the water's category, whether the basement is finished, how long it sat wet, how many days of equipment it takes to dry, and how much carpet, pad, and drywall has to come out.

Square footage scales the extraction and drying cost in a fairly direct way, since both are priced by the area that got wet. Finished basements almost always cost more than unfinished ones, because drywall, baseboard, flooring, and sometimes wall insulation all sit in the path of the water and have to be assessed, dried, or removed.

How long the water sat matters more than people expect. Drywall and insulation start absorbing within hours, and [mold can begin growing within 24 to 48 hours](https://www.epa.gov/mold/brief-guide-mold-moisture-and-your-home) on wet material. If cleanup starts the same day, more of the basement can often be dried in place. If it sits for two days, more material usually has to come out instead.

Drying equipment and days are billed on top of extraction. A typical basement needs an air mover roughly every 10 to 16 linear feet plus one or two low-grain-refrigerant dehumidifiers, run for 3 to 5 days under the [ANSI/IICRC S500 drying standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/). Carpet pad almost never gets reused once it has been wet; carpet can sometimes be saved if the water was clean and it was pulled up and dried fast. Drywall that was contaminated or soaked too long gets cut 12 to 24 inches above the waterline, with the wet insulation behind it removed, which also sets the repaint and rehang cost later.

## Does clean water cost less to clean up than groundwater or sewage?

Yes. Category 1 clean water, like a burst supply line or failed water heater, usually costs the least because materials can often be dried in place instead of torn out. Groundwater seeping through a foundation under hydrostatic pressure is classified as Category 2 and usually requires some porous materials to come out rather than just dry. Sewage, a sewer backup, or storm water flowing in from outside, including a window well that overtops during heavy rain, is Category 3 and needs full antimicrobial treatment plus PPE for the crew, which is the most expensive tier of cleanup.

That last distinction catches a lot of Saratoga Springs homeowners off guard. A window well that fills and pushes surface water into a finished basement during a fall storm is treated the same as a sewage loss for cleanup purposes, not the same as a clean supply-line leak, even though no sewage was involved.

## How much does basement water removal (extraction) cost by itself?

Standalone water extraction, pumping or vacuuming out standing water before drying begins, typically runs $300 to $800 per visit for a residential basement, depending on how many inches of water are present and whether a submersible pump is needed. Extraction is usually the first line item on a larger cleanup job rather than a separate service, and it may need to repeat if water is still actively coming in, such as a window well that has not been diverted yet or an unresolved pipe leak.

## Will homeowners insurance cover basement flood cleanup?

It depends entirely on the water's source. [Most standard homeowners policies cover sudden, accidental water damage](https://www.iii.org/article/which-disasters-are-covered-by-homeowners-insurance) from something like a burst pipe or a failed appliance line. Groundwater or surface water, including flooding from outside the home, generally requires a separate flood policy through the [National Flood Insurance Program or a private flood carrier](https://www.floodsmart.gov/get-insured/buy-a-policy), since standard policies exclude flood. A sump pump failure or sewer backup is typically only covered if the policy has a water backup endorsement added, not as part of standard coverage.

Whichever category applies, document the loss before anything gets removed: photograph the standing water depth, the soaked materials, and keep any moisture meter readings. That documentation, along with starting mitigation promptly, protects the claim regardless of which coverage applies. ## What happens during a basement flood cleanup job?

1. **Extraction.** Standing water is pumped or vacuumed out, often with a submersible pump for deeper water.
2. **Moisture mapping and category classification.** A technician checks affected materials with a moisture meter and identifies the water category, which determines how the rest of the job is scoped.
3. **Drying setup.** Air movers and dehumidifiers are placed and typically run 3 to 5 days.
4. **Material removal, if needed.** Saturated carpet pad, insulation, or drywall above the flood-cut line come out.
5. **Antimicrobial treatment,** applied for Category 2 and 3 losses before rebuild begins.
6. **Rebuild.** Drywall, baseboard, flooring, and paint are restored to match.
