Commercial losses carry a different clock than residential ones: lease terms, payroll, inventory, and liability all start accruing cost the moment the water or smoke hits the building. ## What commercial restoration actually involves

Commercial restoration covers the same physics as a residential loss, water migrating through wall cavities, soot settling into HVAC returns, but at a different scale and with more stakeholders. A single-tenant office might need drying equipment sized for an open floor plan and a raised-access computer room. A multi-tenant retail strip might need phased mitigation so three units stay closed while two stay open. Equipment includes commercial-grade desiccant dehumidifiers for large or unheated spaces, negative air machines for odor and soot containment, and extraction gear rated for concrete slab and commercial carpet tile rather than residential pad. Timelines run longer than a typical home loss because commercial buildings have more square footage, more systems (fire suppression, HVAC, electrical panels), and more people who need sign-off before work starts or the space reopens.

## Our process

1. **Scope of loss and stakeholder walkthrough.** We assess the affected space with the property manager, tenant, or ownership present, and identify structural systems, electrical, and HVAC components that need isolation before work begins.
2. **Containment and phased mitigation.** Where only part of a building is affected, we contain that area so unaffected units or departments can keep operating during the work.
3. **Industrial drying or soot and odor remediation.** Depending on the loss type, this means commercial dehumidification and air movement for water, or thermal fogging, HEPA air scrubbing, and surface cleaning for soot and smoke.
4. **Documentation for insurance and business interruption.** We photograph and log affected materials, equipment placement, and moisture or air quality readings throughout the job, since commercial claims frequently include business interruption coverage that requires a clear timeline.
5. **Reconstruction and occupancy sign-off.** Where materials need to be replaced, we handle the repair scope and coordinate final walkthroughs so the space can reopen or be re-leased.

## What separates a good commercial restoration response from a bad one

The most common mistake on commercial jobs is treating the building like an oversized house. A crew that doesn't isolate the HVAC return before drying starts can spread soot or moisture-laden air into units that were never touched by the original loss, turning a one-suite problem into a building-wide one. Another frequent miss is skipping documentation that commercial insurance actually requires: business interruption claims often hinge on a dated, photographed record of when the space became unusable and when it was cleared to reopen, not just before-and-after mitigation photos. Adjusters on commercial claims also look closely at whether drying or soot removal followed a documented standard rather than a visual guess, since commercial policies tend to scrutinize scope more heavily than residential ones. A good response plans the phasing with the tenant or property manager up front instead of improvising around their schedule mid-job.

Commercial restoration costs vary more than residential work because they depend on square footage, number of affected units, and how much of the building needs to stay operational during the job. ## Seasonal & regional considerations

Saratoga Springs winters bring freeze-thaw cycles that stress commercial plumbing the same way they stress residential lines, and a frozen or burst supply line in a vacant suite or storage area can go unnoticed over a long weekend or holiday closure. Flat commercial roofs and rooftop HVAC units are also more exposed to ice damming and wind-driven snow than typical residential rooflines, which can lead to slow leaks into ceiling tile and insulation that aren't obvious until a tenant reports a stain. Summer brings its own risk with cooling system condensation lines backing up in buildings that run air conditioning hard during Utah's dry, hot stretch.
